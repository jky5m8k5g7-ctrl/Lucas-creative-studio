# Last Drip

_Waiting for Lucas at the concept gate · project TEST_SOLACE_2_

## Lucas's idea

> Solace makes a $38 ceramic pour-over dripper, glazed by hand, one cup at a time. The ten minutes of making coffee by hand are the only part of a parent's morning nobody interrupts.

## Developed

**For ten minutes every school morning, a parent's kids wait behind a strip of painter's tape across the kitchen doorway, clutching their emergencies, until the last drip falls through the hand-glazed Solace dripper.**

Format: ad_spot. Ad spot: the idea makes one point, that these are the only ten minutes nobody interrupts. It has one payoff: after the last drip, the parent goes to the kids who have been waiting. That fits in 60 s. Runner-up: a social series (3 × 20 s, with a different interruption held at the tape each morning). It lost because the joke is finding out the whole house is waiting, and that only surprises once. The cutdowns still borrow the series idea of building each piece around one object. A brand film about the hand-glazing lost because the idea's line is about the parent's morning, not about how the dripper is made.

A school morning. Across the kitchen doorway runs a strip of blue painter's tape, laid over older strips gone grey and scuffed where toes have stood. The kettle clicks. A parent sets the hand-glazed Solace dripper on a chipped mug and starts pouring by hand. The seven-year-old arrives first, one shoe on, the other held out with its lace in a knot, toes exactly on the tape. The thirteen-year-old comes next with a permission slip, a pen, and the parent's phone buzzing in her other hand. The small toes creep over the line; without looking up from the phone, she hooks them back behind it with her foot. Nobody speaks: the pour, the drip, one long sigh from the doorway, the phone buzzing until it gives up. The parent doesn't turn around; they know exactly who is there. The last drip falls. The parent lifts the dripper off the mug, sets it on the counter, turns, and takes the first sip facing the tape. Then they step over it, cup in hand, into everybody talking at once, and the used dripper sits on the counter in the only quiet spot left in the house.

What makes it this idea and nobody else's:

- Kept: 'The ten minutes of making coffee by hand are the only part of a parent's morning nobody interrupts.' The coffee is made by hand, and nobody interrupts it.
- Obvious version: a frazzled parent gets away from the kitchen chaos for a slow, quiet pour-over and a peaceful first sip while the house roars off-screen.
- Differs by what it costs someone: the interruptions are not off-screen. They stand at a tape line holding a knotted shoe, a permission slip and a buzzing phone, and they choose to wait.
- Product role: any dripper would give the spot its clock, so the drip alone would make the product decoration. Instead the film ends on the one thing in the kitchen that was made the same slow way the coffee was: the used dripper under 'Solace. Glazed by hand.', the only words on screen. This breaks 'restraint over explanation' on purpose, because the picture can't show hand-glazing. The line is a verified fact, not a claim, and it is not a voiceover. Swap in a mass-made plastic or glass dripper and the last frame has nothing to say. 'One cup at a time' stays off the card until A01 is answered.
- Detail Lucas didn't write: the painter's tape across the doorway, a fresh blue strip on top of older strips gone grey and scuffed where toes have stood, so the rule reads as years old without a word (A07; taste: one real detail per scene; restraint over explanation).
- Rule shown, not told: the teenager hooks the small toes back behind the tape without looking up from the phone. The kids keep the rule themselves, which stops the tape reading as a parent shutting them out (A07).
- Build: live action assumed (A09). 1 location. 3 fictional on-camera roles (parent, child about 7, teen about 13; A03) and 3 consistent characters. No voiceover or scripted lines; the closing chatter is background only. 0 lip-synced lines. 0 generated shots. About 5 shots of hands with the product (filter, pour, lift, set down, first sip; the filter shot depends on A02). 1 end-frame line. No product reference supplied (A02).
- Make-or-break (A07): the doorway, not the drip. With no words, tape on a floor has to read as a rule the kids keep, not a half-finished paint job and not a parent shutting children out. First test: a silent 10 s mock-up in a real doorway with layered real tape, two stand-ins and the hook-back. Strangers who haven't read the brief answer 'why are they standing there?'. It passes if they say something like 'they're waiting for the parent to finish'. Any answer about paint or punishment switches to A07's fallback.
- Second test (A06, a risk but not the make-or-break): a silent phone mock-up with a stand-in ceramic dripper on an opaque mug. Viewers who haven't read the brief say 'now' when they think the brew has ended. If they miss the last drip, the lift carries the cue alone, and the premise already has that beat.
- Cost flag (children, in the waiting beats): cheaper version shoots them as feet on the tape and hands holding objects, with faces only in the closing chatter. The hook-back is feet only.
- Cost flag (hands pouring hot water, in the pour beat): cheaper version uses one continuous pour take and builds the cutaways from the kids waiting, not from more product setups.
- Alternative (not briefed): as the parent steps over the tape, the teenager's other parent steps behind it holding their own mug. The house passes the ten minutes on, one cup at a time.

Story:
